Component 6: Answering My Historical Question with Primary Sources

Historical Question

What was the impact of television news coverage of the 1963 Birmingham Campaign on public and congressional support for the Civil Rights Act of 1964?

PRIMARY SOURCE #1

Radio and Television Report to the American People on Civil Rights, 11 June 1963

How I verified this source
I searched for President Kennedy’s June 11, 1963 civil rights speech and looked for a copy held by an archive instead of a quote site. That led me to the John F. Kennedy Presidential Library and Museum, which owns the original recording and lists it in its White House Audio Collection under the ID JFKWHA-194-001. I checked that the catalog record gives the same title, the date June 11, 1963, and a length of 13 minutes, and that it marks the recording as public domain. The JFK Library is reputable because it’s run by the National Archives, the federal agency that keeps official government records.
Image of the source
Who created the source?
President John F. Kennedy gave the speech, and the White House recorded it. The recording is part of the White House Audio Collection, which means it was made by the government as part of the president’s official work.
What is the source?
It’s a 13-minute audio recording of a speech Kennedy gave live on radio and TV to the whole country. He starts with the University of Alabama, where the federal government had to step in that day so two Black students could enroll. Then he calls civil rights a moral issue and asks Congress to pass laws protecting the right to vote, the right to go to public schools, and the right to be served at places open to the public, like hotels, restaurants, theaters, and stores. He also brings up Birmingham by name, saying, “The events in Birmingham and elsewhere have so increased the cries for equality that no city or state or legislative body can prudently choose to ignore them.”
When was the source created?
The evening of June 11, 1963, about five weeks after Birmingham police turned fire hoses and dogs on young marchers in early May.
Where was it found or located?
Kennedy gave the speech from the Oval Office in the White House. I found the recording in the online digital archives of the John F. Kennedy Presidential Library and Museum in Boston, Massachusetts.
Why is the source important?
This was the first time Kennedy went on national TV and fully committed himself to civil rights, calling it a moral crisis instead of just a legal problem. The legislation he asks for in this speech turned into the bill that became the Civil Rights Act of 1964. Hearing it helps me understand how the president himself explained the moment to Americans in their living rooms, and that Birmingham was on his mind when he did it.
How this source answers my question
This speech shows the government side of my question, which is how Birmingham turned into a push for a law. Kennedy names Birmingham directly and says no legislative body can ignore what happened there, which tells me the national attention from the protests was putting pressure on Congress. The timing matters too, since he gave this speech only weeks after the images from Birmingham were all over the news. He also used television to deliver his answer, so the same medium that showed people Birmingham was now used to ask them to support a civil rights bill. It doesn’t prove TV coverage alone caused the change, but it shows the president treated Birmingham as a turning point.
Link to the primary source
https://www.jfklibrary.org/asset-viewer/archives/jfkwha-194-001

PRIMARY SOURCE #2

[Firemen turn their hoses on civil rights protesters, Birmingham, Alabama, 1963]

How I verified this source
I searched the Library of Congress for photographs of the Birmingham protests from 1963 and found this one in its Prints and Photographs Division. The catalog record lists the photographer as Bruce Davidson, gives the date as 1963 and the place as Birmingham, Alabama, and explains that the title in brackets was written by Library staff. I also saw that the Library showed this photo in its exhibit The Civil Rights Act of 1964: A Long Struggle for Freedom, which connects it directly to my topic. The Library of Congress is reputable because it’s the national library of the United States and has one of the largest collections of historical photographs in the world.
Image of the source
Who created the source?
Bruce Davidson, a photographer with the Magnum Photos agency, took this photo. He spent a lot of time in the early 1960s photographing the civil rights movement in the South.
What is the source?
It’s a black and white photograph, printed as a gelatin silver print, of a protest at Kelly Ingram Park in Birmingham. Firemen are aiming high pressure hoses at Black marchers to force them to break up and leave the park. The title in brackets isn’t Davidson’s, because Library of Congress staff wrote it to describe what the photo shows.
When was the source created?
The Library of Congress record dates it to 1963. It doesn’t give an exact day, but the Birmingham police and fire department turned hoses on marchers in early May 1963, during the Birmingham Campaign.
Where was it found or located?
The photo was taken in Kelly Ingram Park in Birmingham, Alabama, across the street from the Sixteenth Street Baptist Church, where the marches started. The print is held by the Library of Congress Prints and Photographs Division in Washington, D.C., and I found it in the Library’s online catalog.
Why is the source important?
This photo shows the exact kind of scene that cameras captured in Birmingham and that people across the country saw on the news and in magazines. It lets me see for myself what viewers would have been reacting to, instead of just reading descriptions of it. The fact that the Library of Congress picked it for its Civil Rights Act exhibit shows that historians see these images as part of the story of how the law got passed.
How this source answers my question
This photograph shows me what the coverage of Birmingham actually looked like, which is the starting point of my question. It’s a still photo, not TV footage, but it shows the same fire hose attacks that news cameras were filming in Kelly Ingram Park that spring. Seeing firemen use hoses on peaceful marchers makes it easy to understand why viewers who had never been to the South were shocked. That shock is what the Library of Congress connects to the push for the Civil Rights Act by including the photo in its exhibit on the law. Together with Kennedy’s speech, it helps me trace a path from images of Birmingham to public pressure to a bill in Congress.
Link to the primary source
https://www.loc.gov/item/2014645234/
