Research Portfolio Component 5

Answering My Historical Question with Secondary Sources

Historical Question

What was the impact of television news coverage of the 1963 Birmingham Campaign on public and congressional support for the Civil Rights Act of 1964?

SECONDARY SOURCE #1

Equal Time: Television and the Civil Rights Movement

Aniko Bodroghkozy

Author’s argument
Network television played a crucial role during the civil rights movement “in reconfiguring a new ‘common sense’ about race relations.”
Fill in the blanks
In Equal Time: Television and the Civil Rights Movement, Aniko Bodroghkozy argues that network television played a crucial role “in reconfiguring a new ‘common sense’ about race relations.”
How this answers my question
Bodroghkozy shows that by the early 1960s nearly every American household had a television, and the civil rights struggle became network news’s first major ongoing domestic story. She explains that King and other leaders deliberately created confrontations that would draw cameras, and Birmingham, with its police dogs and fire hoses turned on schoolchildren, is one of the clearest examples. Networks often built their reports in ways that supported integration, so viewers across the country saw the movement framed sympathetically. This helps answer my question because it explains how coverage could shift public opinion and build pressure on Congress to pass the Civil Rights Act. At the same time, she argues that television’s role was more complicated than it seems, since segregationists also fought for airtime.
Link to the source
https://muse.jhu.edu/book/21570

SECONDARY SOURCE #2

“Breakthrough in Birmingham”

For All the World to See: Visual Culture and the Struggle for Civil Rights by Maurice Berger

Author’s argument
The broadcasting and publication of the images of racial violence in Birmingham “represented a turning point in the civil rights movement.”
Fill in the blanks
In “Breakthrough in Birmingham,” Maurice Berger argues that the broadcasting and publication of images of racial violence in Birmingham in 1963 “represented a turning point in the civil rights movement.”
How this answers my question
Berger shows that movement leaders turned the Birmingham Campaign into a growing visual spectacle and relied on photographers and television crews to spread the images. Footage of Black protesters attacked by dogs, beaten with clubs, and sprayed with high pressure hoses appeared across many media outlets. In the months after this coverage, national surveys showed that white Americans’ support for civil rights increased. He also notes that a major Southern newspaper credited these pictures in 1964 with building the momentum that pushed the Civil Rights Act through Congress. This directly answers my question because it links the televised images from Birmingham to both a shift in public opinion and the passage of the law.
Link to the source
https://fatwts.umbc.edu/breakthrough-in-birmingham/
