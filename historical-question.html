Developing My Historical Question

Step 1: Brainstorm

Topics I’m interested in

  • Film and television, since I work in video production doing camera and lighting.
  • The civil rights movement.
  • The Vietnam War and how Americans experienced it at home.
  • How new technology changes the way people get information.

My research goal I want to understand how moving images, not just words, have changed what Americans believed and how they acted. Because I set up shots for a living, I’m curious whether the camera has ever changed the course of history.

Time period I’m interested in The 1950s through the 1970s. Television became part of almost every American home in this era, and it overlapped with the civil rights movement and the Vietnam War.

Step 2: Narrow My Research Question

Aspect: social, political, cultural, technological, or economic? My topic touches technology and culture, but what I really want to know is whether TV changed people’s minds and government decisions. That makes it mostly a political question with a social side.

Specific group, community, or organization At first I considered “the American public,” but that’s too broad. I narrowed it to civil rights activists in Birmingham, Alabama, and the white Northern viewers who watched them on the evening news. The Southern Christian Leadership Conference, led by Martin Luther King Jr., planned the Birmingham Campaign partly with news cameras in mind.

Specific time period I started with “the civil rights era,” roughly 1954 to 1968. That’s fourteen years, too much to cover well. I narrowed it to spring 1963 through July 2, 1964: from the Birmingham protests, when police used fire hoses and dogs on young marchers, to the signing of the Civil Rights Act.

Step 3: Write My Question

I tried several question stems to see which fit best:

  1. Why was television historically important? Way too broad. This could be a whole book.
  2. What were the consequences of television coverage of the civil rights movement? Better, but the movement lasted years and had many campaigns.
  3. In what ways did public opinion on civil rights change between 1963 and 1964? Specific in time, but it drops the part I care about most, which is television.
  4. What was the impact of television news coverage of the 1963 Birmingham Campaign on public and congressional support for the Civil Rights Act of 1964? This connects one event, one medium, and one outcome. This is my question.

Step 4: Test My Question

Is it clear? Yes. I read it aloud to check the grammar and wording. It names exactly what I’m studying: TV news coverage, the Birmingham Campaign, and support for the Civil Rights Act.

Is it achievable? Yes. I can use:

  • Gallup polls from 1963 and 1964 on civil rights.
  • Archived news footage and photographs from Birmingham.
  • President Kennedy’s June 11, 1963 civil rights address, which came just weeks after Birmingham.
  • The Congressional Record and newspaper coverage of the Civil Rights Act debate.
  • Scholarly books and articles on media and the civil rights movement, available through the library database.

Is it precise? Mostly. One risk is that it’s hard to prove television alone caused support to change, since other events also mattered, like the March on Washington and Kennedy’s assassination. When I answer the question, I’ll treat television coverage as one major factor and compare it with these other events, rather than claiming it was the only cause.

My Final Historical Question

What was the impact of television news coverage of the 1963 Birmingham Campaign on public and congressional support for the Civil Rights Act of 1964?
