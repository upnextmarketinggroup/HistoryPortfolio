Final Findings

Answering My Historical Question with Primary and Secondary Sources

Historical Question

What was the impact of television news coverage of the 1963 Birmingham Campaign on public and congressional support for the Civil Rights Act of 1964?

Introduction

For my research project, I looked at the Birmingham Campaign of 1963 and the television news coverage of it. The main people involved are the civil rights activists in Birmingham, Alabama, many of them young marchers, and the Southern Christian Leadership Conference, which was led by Martin Luther King Jr. On the other side were the Birmingham police and firemen who used dogs and fire hoses on the marchers. President John F. Kennedy and Congress also play a big part, along with the white viewers in the North who saw the protests on the evening news. My time period starts in spring 1963, when the hoses and dogs were used in early May, and ends on July 2, 1964, when the Civil Rights Act was signed. The protests happened in the South, mostly around Kelly Ingram Park and the Sixteenth Street Baptist Church, but people all over the country watched them. My historical question is: What was the impact of television news coverage of the 1963 Birmingham Campaign on public and congressional support for the Civil Rights Act of 1964? My argument is that the coverage was a turning point. After Americans saw police attacking peaceful marchers on TV, more white Americans started supporting civil rights, and Kennedy asked Congress for the law that became the Civil Rights Act. I don’t think television was the only cause, but my sources show it was a big one.

The Sources

My two primary sources were President Kennedy’s “Radio and Television Report to the American People on Civil Rights,” a recording from June 11, 1963 held by the JFK Library, and a 1963 photograph by Bruce Davidson of firemen spraying protesters with hoses in Birmingham, from the Library of Congress. My two secondary sources were Aniko Bodroghkozy’s book Equal Time: Television and the Civil Rights Movement and Maurice Berger’s “Breakthrough in Birmingham” from For All the World to See. Davidson’s photo shows what viewers were actually reacting to. Berger says images like this one showed up across many media outlets, and that national surveys afterward showed white support for civil rights going up. Kennedy gave his speech about five weeks after the hoses were used, and he mentions Birmingham by name, saying no legislative body “can prudently choose to ignore” what happened. Berger also points out that a major Southern newspaper said in 1964 that these pictures helped get the law through Congress. The photo, the speech, and Berger all point to the same path, from images to public pressure to a new law. Bodroghkozy mostly agrees, since she says TV played a role “in reconfiguring a new ‘common sense’ about race relations,” but she thinks it was more complicated because segregationists were also fighting for airtime. Kennedy’s speech is a little different too. He starts with the University of Alabama and talks about “Birmingham and elsewhere,” so to him Birmingham was one event out of several.

Conclusion and Reflection

There is still a lot my research doesn’t cover. I planned on using Gallup polls from 1963 and 1964 and the Congressional Record, but I never analyzed them myself, so what I say about public opinion and Congress comes from Berger’s summary of the surveys and from Kennedy’s speech. My photo is also a still image, not TV footage, so I haven’t looked at the actual broadcasts. Some voices are missing as well, like the marchers telling their own story, white Southerners who watched the news, the segregationists Bodroghkozy mentions, and the members of Congress who voted on the bill. Events like the March on Washington and Kennedy’s assassination also affected support for the law, and I can’t say how much they mattered compared to TV. A future historian could compare poll numbers to the dates the footage aired, or go through the network footage from May 1963. If I kept working on this, I would read the Congressional Record debates and look for accounts from people who marched. I also see a connection to today. In 1963, people who had never been to Birmingham saw the violence on TV and started paying attention. Now viral videos on social media work in a similar way, since people can see things happen in places they have never been, and that puts pressure on lawmakers. One big difference is that back then a few TV networks chose what everyone saw, and today anyone with a phone can film and post it.
