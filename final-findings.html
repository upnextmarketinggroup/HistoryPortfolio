Final Findings

Answering My Historical Question with Primary and Secondary Sources

Historical Question

What was the impact of television news coverage of the 1963 Birmingham Campaign on public and congressional support for the Civil Rights Act of 1964?

Introduction

My research focuses on the Birmingham Campaign of 1963 and the role television news played in what came after it. The main people in this story are the civil rights activists of Birmingham, Alabama, including the young marchers and the Southern Christian Leadership Conference led by Martin Luther King Jr.; the Birmingham police and firemen who turned dogs and fire hoses on them; President John F. Kennedy; Congress; and the white Northern viewers who watched it all on the evening news. The time period runs from spring 1963, when police used hoses and dogs on marchers in early May, to July 2, 1964, when the Civil Rights Act was signed. The protests took place in the Deep South, in Birmingham around Kelly Ingram Park and the Sixteenth Street Baptist Church, but the audience was national. My historical question is: What was the impact of television news coverage of the 1963 Birmingham Campaign on public and congressional support for the Civil Rights Act of 1964? Based on my sources, my argument is that television coverage of Birmingham was a major turning point. By bringing images of police violence against peaceful marchers into living rooms across the country, it helped increase white Americans’ support for civil rights and pushed President Kennedy to ask Congress for the law that became the Civil Rights Act. Television was not the only cause, but it created pressure that lawmakers could not ignore.

The Sources

In my research, I analyzed two primary sources: President Kennedy’s “Radio and Television Report to the American People on Civil Rights,” a recording from June 11, 1963 held by the JFK Library, and Bruce Davidson’s 1963 photograph of firemen turning their hoses on protesters in Birmingham, held by the Library of Congress. My secondary sources were Aniko Bodroghkozy’s book Equal Time: Television and the Civil Rights Movement and Maurice Berger’s “Breakthrough in Birmingham” from For All the World to See. Davidson’s photo shows the kind of scene viewers were reacting to, and Berger confirms that images like it spread across many media outlets and were followed by a rise in white support for civil rights in national surveys. Kennedy’s speech, given about five weeks after the hoses were used, shows the government responding. He names Birmingham directly and says no legislative body “can prudently choose to ignore” it. Berger adds that a major Southern newspaper credited the pictures in 1964 with pushing the law through Congress, linking all of these sources into one chain. The sources do not agree on everything, though. Berger calls the images a clear turning point, while Bodroghkozy agrees television helped in “reconfiguring a new ‘common sense’ about race relations” but argues its role was more complicated, since segregationists also fought for airtime. Kennedy also opens his speech with the University of Alabama and speaks of “Birmingham and elsewhere,” treating Birmingham as one event among several, while the photo and Berger focus on it alone.

Conclusion and Reflection

My research still has gaps. I planned to use Gallup polls from 1963 and 1964 and the Congressional Record, but I did not analyze them directly, so my claims about public opinion and Congress rely on Berger’s summary of national surveys and on Kennedy’s speech. I also used a still photograph instead of actual TV footage, so I have not studied the broadcasts themselves. Several perspectives are left out, including the Birmingham marchers in their own words, white Southern viewers, the segregationists Bodroghkozy says fought for airtime, and the members of Congress who voted on the bill. Other forces, like the March on Washington and Kennedy’s assassination, also shaped support for the law, and I have not measured how much each one mattered compared to television. Future historians could line up poll results with broadcast dates or study the May 1963 network footage itself. If I expanded this project, I would add the Congressional Record debates and accounts from people who marched. The pattern I found still feels current. In 1963, people who had never been to Birmingham saw the violence on their TV screens and started to pay attention. Today, viral videos shared on social media do something similar, letting people far away see events for themselves and putting pressure on lawmakers to respond. The difference is that in 1963 a few networks decided what the country saw, while now anyone with a phone can record and share it, spreading that power across millions of people.
